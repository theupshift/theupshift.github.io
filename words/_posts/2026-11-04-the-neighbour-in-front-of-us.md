“Naogopa kurogwa,” “ni wezi,” “wanajizoesha,” “it’s better to give to charities”—and other petty excuses we give for not helping the stranger begging right in front of us.

These are everyday excuses I, too, sometimes find myself making. We give at church and to charities, convincing ourselves that perhaps that is enough, or even the better way to give. There is something reassuring about giving through an institution: we can choose a cause, make a contribution, and trust that someone better placed will decide how to use it. And there are good reasons to support organisations doing important work. But does that mean we should always walk past the person asking us for help on the street?

I recently read a thought-provoking essay by Sigal Samuel, [“I give to charity — but never to people on the street. Is that wrong?”](https://wallhop.io/https://www.vox.com/future-perfect/372519/charity-giving-effective-altruism-mutual-aid-homeless) The full essay is available through that link as a gift. Samuel asks whether, in our quest to give effectively, we have forgotten how to simply care.

The question at the heart of the essay is a familiar one: why not get the biggest bang for your buck? If one donation can prevent more deaths or relieve more suffering than another, surely we should pay attention to that. Effective giving has its place. We should care whether our contributions make a difference, and whether organisations use resources well.

But the difficulty begins when we treat every form of human need as if it could be measured on the same scale. A donation that helps prevent malaria, support a local shelter, or provide a meal to someone who has not eaten may produce very different kinds of good. Some benefits can be counted; others are harder to quantify. Human dignity, relief from shame, companionship, and the feeling that someone has noticed you do not always fit neatly into a cost-effectiveness calculation.

Not every act of kindness needs to be optimised. Sometimes, helping someone right in front of you is reason enough.

## The cost of becoming too calculating

I particularly enjoyed Samuel’s reference to philosopher [Bernard Williams](https://plato.stanford.edu/ENTRIES/williams-bernard/) and his critique of utilitarianism. Williams’s concern with personal integrity was, in part, about what happens when an abstract moral theory asks us to set aside the commitments and attachments that make us who we are. We can become alienated from a core part of ourselves when we continually override our instinct to respond to another person’s suffering in pursuit of some supposedly greater good elsewhere.

That idea stayed with me. The part of us that notices someone in distress is not necessarily an irrational distraction from doing good. It may be the very source of our desire to help in the first place. If we silence it every time a calculation points elsewhere, we may become more efficient at giving while becoming less responsive to the people around us.

This is not an argument against thoughtful or effective philanthropy. Nor does it mean that every request must be met with money. We may have legitimate concerns about safety, uncertainty, manipulation, or whether cash is the most useful form of help. We can set boundaries and still remain compassionate. We can give carefully without allowing caution to harden into indifference.

Perhaps the answer is not to choose between organised charity and direct giving, but to make room for both. We can support organisations with a strong record of doing good and still keep something aside for the person we encounter on the way home. We can be deliberate about our giving without turning every human interaction into an audit of expected returns.

## Who is my neighbour?

The essay also brought to mind [Jesus’s parable of the Good Samaritan in Luke 10:25–37](https://www.bible.com/bible/111/LUK.10.25-37.NIV). The question put to Jesus was, “And who is my neighbour?” In the story, a priest and a Levite see an injured man and pass by on the other side. A Samaritan stops, tends to his wounds, and makes arrangements for his care. Jesus then turns the question around: who acted as a neighbour to the man who had been attacked?

The point is not that institutions, distant causes, or careful planning do not matter. It is that compassion cannot remain an abstract commitment. At some point, it asks something of us in the presence of another person. The stranger in front of us is not merely an item in a larger calculation.

Perhaps the question is not always how much good we can do elsewhere, but whether we are willing to stop and help the person suffering right in front of us. After all, who is my neighbour?

I am also adding [*Optimal Illusions* by Coco Krumme](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/670624/optimal-illusions-by-coco-krumme/) to my reading list. The book traces how optimisation became such a powerful idea in modern life, and asks what we lose when efficiency becomes the measure of everything. It sounds like a useful companion to Samuel’s essay.

For now, I am left with a simple thought: let us give thoughtfully, support good work, and care about whether our giving helps. But let us not become so preoccupied with finding the most effective way to help that we forget to help the person standing right in front of us.
