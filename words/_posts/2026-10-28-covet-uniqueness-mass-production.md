As [J. Brodsky](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/6-steps-happiness-j-brodsky-sergey-chaplygin) suggests, coveting what others have can come at the cost of our own uniqueness, even as it fuels mass production.

There is an irony in the human desire to possess what belongs to another. We admire a thing, then desire it; we desire it, then pursue it; and in pursuing it, we sometimes abandon the very qualities that make us distinct. What begins as admiration can quietly become imitation, and what begins as inspiration can end in self-erasure.

The problem is not that we desire what others have. Desire is human, and admiration can be instructive. We learn by observing others. We borrow ideas, adopt practices, and improve upon what already exists. Civilisation itself owes much to this exchange of ideas. But there is a difference between learning from another person and aspiring to become their replica.

Covetousness, unlike admiration, is rarely satisfied with appreciating something from a distance. It wants possession. It wants equivalence. And sometimes, it wants superiority. It does not merely say, *That is beautiful.* It says, *I must have it too, or I am somehow less.*

This is where uniqueness begins to suffer.
