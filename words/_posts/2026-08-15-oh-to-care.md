As I clerked him, he told me his story.

A prisoner.  
A former miner.  
A father of nine girls.  
Only 41.

He had come with his first decompensation—grade 3 ascites so tense that it made breathing difficult.

He had been prescribed medication.  
But he had not been taking it.

We tapped him. He breathed easier. He left well.

Later, as I read about cirrhosis, I came across the numbers: nine years can become 1.6.[^1]

Sometimes, optimal care means more time.

That is why care cannot end with prescribing the right medicine—it must ensure that the patient can, and does, take it.

The same principle extends beyond cirrhosis. In heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, comprehensive disease-modifying therapy was projected to add 6.3 years of survival for a typical 55-year-old compared with conventional therapy.[^2] A network meta-analysis similarly found the greatest mortality reduction with comprehensive four-drug therapy.[^3]

The evidence matters.  
But only when it reaches—and stays with—the patient.<!-- more -->
